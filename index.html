06 · How Perplexity Works

The maths, in plain English

🎲 The Weighted Die
A fair 6-sided die has perplexity exactly 6 — every face is equally likely. Rig the die so one face comes up almost always, and perplexity shrinks toward 1. Perplexity is "how many faces is this die effectively rolling?"
🔍 Why 1/P?
If something has a 99% chance, 1 / 0.99 ≈ 1.01 — zero shock. If a 0.1% chance event happens, 1 / 0.001 = 1,000 — a thousand times the surprise. Inverting the probability turns "how likely" into "how shocking".
📐 The N-th Root
Multiplying ten probabilities makes an astronomically tiny number. Taking the N-th root (the geometric mean) normalises by sentence length, so a 3-word sentence and a 30-word sentence can be fairly compared.
🧮 Cross-entropy
Average surprise in bits: H = −(log₂P₁ + … + log₂Pₙ) / N. And the neat identity: perplexity = 2^H. Same number, two languages.

Core glossary

Token
The atomic fragment of language the model sees — a word, a subword, or punctuation. GPT-2's vocabulary has 50,257 tokens.
Log-softmax
The model's raw scores are turned into probabilities in log space, because exponentiating raw numbers can overflow. Stable maths, same answers.
Start token 50256
The GPT-2 <|endoftext|> symbol. We put it in front so the very first word gets a real probability too.
Surprise (bits)
−log₂P: how many yes/no questions the word was "worth". A word with P = 1/8 carries exactly 3 bits of surprise.

Questions, answered

Why does “Python love I” score so much higher than “I love Python”?

Same words, different order. DistilGPT-2 learned English word order from millions of documents, so a scrambled sentence breaks its expectations — the final word gets an extremely low probability, and one tiny P drags the whole average up.

Does my sentence leave my computer?

No. The model weights and the tokenizer download once into your browser, and every calculation happens on your machine. Nothing you type is sent anywhere.

What does a perplexity of 8 mean?

On average, the model was as unsure as rolling an 8-sided die — as if it were choosing at random between 8 equally likely words at every step.

Why is the real model's number so much bigger than the simulator's 2.32?

The simulator uses friendly made-up probabilities like 0.5 and 0.8. The real model scores real words — and it is brutally honest: in “I love Python” it gives “Python” a probability of about 0.00006, which alone multiplies the perplexity by 15,650. Toy numbers are kind; reality is not.
